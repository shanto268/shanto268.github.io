Introduction

Driver-less cars (i.e. autonomous vehicles, henceforth called AVs) have already appeared on the road and shared right of way with human-driven vehicles (henceforth called HVs). An appealing feature of AVs is their capability to form platoons, which is anticipated to boost efficiency of traffic flow substantially. A number of recent research have delved into modeling and operations of the mixed flow of AVs and HVs, most of them attempted to capture or optimize the platooning feature of AVs.

Much attention in literature (see e.g. [@bose2003analysis][@talebpour2016influence][@olia2018traffic][@wang2014rolling][@gong2016constrained][@zhou2017parsimonious]) has focused on problems along the longitudinal dimension and adopt a microscopic approach, which embraces simulation of car-following behaviors, platoon control, and stability analysis. While the understanding of microscopic longitudinal behaviors of mixed AV-HV flow has become relatively mature, the collective behaviors to be anticipated in a multi-lane setting remain elusive, where the interplay of AVs and HVs can be substantially more complicated, due to the possibility of lane choice and lane changing. The lateral behaviors are coupled with longitudinal dynamics, and the two together determine dynamics of mixed AV-HV flow. Discussions along this line are still primitive, mostly focused on the estimation of mixed flow capacity (see e.g. [@chen2017towards][@ghiasi2017mixed]) and aggregate flow-density relation (see e.g. [@rao1993flow][@levin2016multiclass]). These works all assume the mixed flow is in perfect steady state does not explicitly consider lateral interactions in general multilane setting. Concerning the impacts of lanes on traffic flow, several empirical studies [@menendez2007effects][@daganzo2008effects][@cassidy2009spatiotemporal][@cassidy2010smoothing] have been conducted towards understanding the impacts of HOV lanes on traffic in general purpose (GP) lanes, which revealed the existence of a smoothing effect caused by restriction of lane-changing. Past research also examines lane-to-lane traffic interactions on multilane highways, which include lane flow distribution [@michalopoulos1984multilane][@shiomi2015multilane], as well as impacts of microscopic lane-changes [@laval2006lc][@ahn2007freeway]. On the theoretical side, it is only in limited cases that the connection between lane policy and traffic flow characteristics has been investigated, and in almost all studies, the policy considered is static. [@daganzo1997continuum] and [@jin2018new] both considered traffic consisting of regular and priority vehicles, and a number of special lanes are accessible only to the priority vehicles. Both regular vehicles and priority vehicles are endowed with the identical fundamental diagram. Nonetheless, these studies are focused on human-driven traffic flow and do not consider any AV-specific behaviors. [@chen2017towards] discussed capacity of mixed flow of AVs and HVs on multi-lane freeway under different combination of static lane access policies, assuming traffic flow is steady. [@ghiasi2017mixed] derived capacity of mixed AV-HV flow in a similar context, assuming the spatial distribution of mixed flow follows a Markov process. An interesting lateral policy considered in literature is intermittent bus lane (IBL), which offers priority to buses, but also allows other regular vehicles to use the lane when space is available. [@eichler2006bus] and [@chiabaut2012road] conducted queuing analysis using the LWR model and derived corresponding roadway capacity as well as conditions when IBL will benefit the entire system.

The key challenge to model mixed flow dynamics of AVs and HVs lies in capturing agents’ longitudinal and lateral behaviors simultaneously, which should account for not only agents’ dynamical characters (e.g. their longitudinal speed and acceleration as functions of headway), but also agents’ decision-making, as well as how the decisions of individual agents aggregate. Such interactions in mixed flow of AVs and HVs is conceivably more complicated than mixed human-driving traffic due to the rich possibilities of AV behaviors. For instance, with the advanced sensing and communication capabilities, AVs may behave more opportunistically than HVs in seeking peers in its neighborhood, because this potentially allow them to form platoons with peers and benefit from more smooth driving and better traveling speed.

In this paper, we are interested in understanding the collective dynamics of AV-HV flow, as a result of the interactions between heterogeneous agents. In particular, we seek to answer the following research questions: when AVs are self-interested and individually controlled by themselves (as opposed to coordinated by system operator), will they be able to self-organize into platoons? And if so, what are the conditions for the self-organization to initiate and sustain? This research is partly inspired from the self-organized phenomena widely existing in biological systems, such as army ants [@couzin2003self], where orders emerge when agents interact with each other locally. In traffic flow literature, similar self-organized phenomena were also reported [@helbing2001traffic].

To answer the above questions, we propose a Cellular Automaton (CA) model to encapsulate the essential behavioral factors of AVs and HVs, and conduct simulation experiments to characterize the impact of these behavior factors on the formation of clusters in mixed traffic flow. The simulation experiments were conducted on a circular road with three lanes. We consider two alternatives of AV behaviors. In the first scenario, AVs behave like HVs, except the difference in free flow speed and braking probability. In the second scenario, AVs are assumed to be fully aware of vehicle types in neighborhood and opportunistic in gap seeking.

The major finding of this research is the self-organized formation of AV clusters and lanes without any centralized control. This finding may suggest clustering as an intrinsic property of mixed flow, when AVs and HVs interact, and AVs are endowed with opportunistic behaviors. The existence of self-organization, if turns out to exist in real mixed flow of AVs and HVs, may suggest the possibility of regulating their interactions through a decentralized approach.

The remaining part of the paper is organized as follows. We first introduce how we capture the different car-following and gap seeking behaviors of AVs and HVs through a Cellular Automata (CA) model. Then we describe the setup of simulation experiments along with results and discussion. In this section, we focus on the flux of mixed flow as well as the formation process of AV clusters in different behavioral scenarios. Self-organized behaviors are observed in both models of AV behavior. We summarize the findings and remark on future works in the last section.

Behavior design and implementation

Cellular Automata (CA) is a simple yet effective framework to model agent interactions over cells, which have found wide and successful applications modeling traffic flow. In CA, each agent occupies a cell, and its behavior is determined by its own state and the state of other agents in its neighborhood. Simple functions can be applied to these cell objects to change its state and as a result it can change the states of neighboring cells [@CAb]. Nagel and Schreckenberg (1992) were amongst the first to use this computational technique to model traffic flow [@CA].

CA is a discrete computational model in traffic flow literature that comprises a system of vehicles which evolve over linear time in accordance to rules (see e.g. [@CA2][@NaSch]) summarized in Fig.[fig:ca]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”fig:ca”} and 8{reference-type=”ref” reference=”ca_rules”}; where, $v_{j}$ represents the speed of the car $j$, $v_{max}$ is the speed limit of the road, $d_{j}$ is the number of empty cells in front of car $j$, $x_{j}$ is the cell that car $j$ occupies, $v_{l’}$ is the number of empty cells in front of car $j$ if it were on lane, $l’$, and $d_{back}$ is the number of empty cells behind in the car in lane $l’$. Each update of the system, requires all the vehicles to follow these rules simultaneously, with each vehicle first deciding whether to change lane before deciding how much to move forward; this order of decision making is fundamental to the CA model.

In this paper, we adapt the Nagel-Schreckenberg Cellular Automaton model to introduce a model of mixed traffic flow of HVs and AVs that captures three potential behaviors of AVs -opportunistic, neighbor awareness and baseline behavior. We distinguish between the two class of vehicles in our simulation by assigning different behavioural parameters - braking probability, lane changing probability, maximum speed - and methods - traversal velocity functions- to each vehicle type and model.

Opportunistic behavior

Autonomous Vehicles require well defined instructions in the form of algorithms and optimization functions to make decisions while driving [@pp:1]. This level of algorithmic control on the decision making of AVs imply that such vehicles can achieve idealized traffic flow parameters that cannot be attained by HVs. This is due to the fact that HVs are prone to velocity fluctuations due to human behavior or due to varying external conditions. This type of erratic behaviour does not apply to AVs, since they are well aware of their surroundings and make their decisions of accelerating/deceleration solely based on safety and opportunity. Similarly, when it comes to changing lanes, AVs will always change lanes given that it is both safe and rewarding to change lanes. HVs, like in previous CA models, behave more "humanly" in this aspect, because it depends on the aggressiveness of the human driver. In order to capture such opportunistic behavior of AV, we propose the following model.

In our model, an AV considers changing lanes given that the Incentive and Safety criteria are met. Then, if AV is purely opportunistic, the following must be true. \(p_{l}(HV) < p_{l}(AV)\) \(p_{s}(HV) > p_{s}(AV)\)

In our model, the $p_{l}(HV)$ and $p_{s}(HV)$ values are such that HV behaviour in our simulation is stochastic and hence realistic, whereas the values for $p_{l}(AV) = 1$ and $p_{s}(AV) = 0$ for AVs were chosen to reflect purely opportunistic behaviour. The traversal velocity update equation that captures the distinct AV behavior under this model is: \(v^{av}(t+1) = min(v^{av}(t)+1, s_{lv}, v_{max}^{av}, v_{sl})\)

Here, the $v^{av}(t)+1$ term represents acceleration.

Neighbor Awareness

Autonomous Vehicles can communicate with each other and the surrounding using V2X technology [@v2v]. This means that is is possible for AVs to be aware of its spatial orientation and location. Furthermore, AVs can also be aware of the location of other nearby AVs and can be in constant communication with each other [@v2x]. We hypothesize that these "neighbor aware" AVs would behave differently depending on the type of vehicle they are trailing. An AV trailing another AV can maintain a shorter headway due to their inter-connectivity and, hence, can maintain a higher speed; such phenomenon would not be seen if the leading vehicle is a HV. We assume HVs to not have any distinct behavior differences that depend on the class of vehicle they follow. In order to model such behaviour, we need to introduce a new definition of $v_{max}$, which traditionally represents the speed limit of the road in CA models. Since, the car is now aware of the type of vehicle it is trailing, we can redefine the maximum speed of the car, $v_{max}$, as follows:

\(\label{eq:v} v_{max}= \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} v_{aa} &\text{if } AV-AV \\ v_{ah} &\text{if } AV-HV \\ v_{h} &\text{if } HV \\ \end{array} \right.\) Thus, if we take into account the smaller headways of AVs trailing other AVs. It follows that: \(\label{eq:cond} v_{aa} > v_{ah} \geq v_{h}\)

From Equations [eq:v]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”eq:v”} and [eq:cond]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”eq:cond”}, it can be seen that an AV following another AV can attain the highest possible speed, $v_{aa}$, whereas the maximum speed for a HV is the lowest, $v_{h}$ and is independent of the class of the vehicle it trails. Throughout the rest of the paper we use the notation $v^L$ to refer to velocity functions that depend on the type of the leading vehicle and obey the inequality in Eqn [eq:cond]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”eq:cond”}. Our model follows the general rules outlined in Fig [fig:ca]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”fig:ca”}, with the some changes to certain rules marked by " " in Fig.[fig:ca]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”fig:ca”}.

To summarize, when an AV exhibits neighbor aware behaviour, it is aware of the type of vehicle in front of it and changes its velocity as a response limiting its maximum speed, $v^L_{max}$, while HV behaves indifferently. The traversal velocity update function for this model is as follows: \(v^{av}(t+1) = min(v^{av}(t)+1, s_{lv}, v_{max}^{L}, v_{sl}) - \delta(p_s(AV))\) Here, the term $v^{av}(t)+1$ represents acceleration and $\delta(p_s(AV))$ is a delta function that can either be 0 or 1 depending on the braking probability of the AV (Eqn. [deltas]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”deltas”}). \(\label{deltas} & \delta(p_s(AV)) = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1 & \text{if } p_s(AV) < X \text{ for $X \sim U[(0,1)]$} \\ 0 & otherwise \\ \end{array} \right.\)

Baseline behavior

In this model AVs behave exactly like an HV, with their behavior being indistinguishable from one another. Traditional CA rules described in Fig.[fig:ca]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”fig:ca”} were implemented to encapsulate such behavior in addition to the following constraints imposed by the uniformity of the two classes. \(\label{eq:b1} p_{l}(HV) = p_{l}(AV)\) \(\label{eq:b2} p_{s}(HV) = p_{s}(AV)\) The braking and lane-change probabilities for both classes of vehicles are the same (Eqns. [eq:b1]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”eq:b1”} [eq:b2]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”eq:b2”}) and they both follow the same traversal velocity update equation (Eqn. [eq:bv]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”eq:bv”}) . \(\label{eq:bv} v(t+1) = min(v(t)+1, s_{lv}, v_{max}, v_{sl}) - \delta(p_s)\)



It should be also be noted that all of these behaviors- opportunistic, neighbor aware baseline- satisfy the safety constraints and correspond to pure opportunistic and pure random nature. Moreover, since, real-world AV behaviors are not well known as of yet due to the lack of large scale deployment of such vehicles, we cannot be sure of the accuracy of our models. Furthermore, for the purpose of simulation experiments, HV behaviors across all AV models were kept uniform with constant parameters - $p_{s}(HV), p_{l}(HV)$- and followed the traditional traversal update equation Eqn. [eq:bv]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”eq:bv”} with varied $v^{hv}_{max}$.

Model Implementation

Both our simulation and analysis program were written in Python3. The graphics for the simulation were made using the Pygame 1.9.6 module. To create our model, we used principles of Object Oriented Programming with the main objects being two Abstract Data Structures: Road and Car. The implementation of the NaSch CA algorithms was done through passing functions to these data structures and manipulating the control flow of the program. 10{reference-type=”ref” reference=”naschprg”} contains link to the GitHub repository for this program.

Simulation experiments and findings

Upon observation of several different simulation cases, we discovered that AVs under certain models and situations displayed strong self-organized phenomenon- they started from a random state and evolved towards states that had the AVs moving closer to one another for prolonged periods of times. There were two types of self-organized events that were most prominent: AVs moving closely together but in different lanes and AVs moving closely together in the same lane. For the purpose of our simulation experiments, we have defined such phenomenona (cluster) as follows: \(\textit{A cluster is a group of 4 or more AVs each at most 3 cells apart.}\)

Such broad definition of a cluster allows us to incorporate both the self-organized platooning and lane-formation events into one collective phenomenon. The ratio that determines the prominence of one form of a cluster over the other depends on the following factors - radius of circular road, proportion of AVs on the road, distribution of HVs and overall traffic density. Lane-formation events, by definition, require undisturbed chains of AVs sharing the same lane; Hence, longer circular roads, higher proportion of AVs, localized distribution of HVs and lower traffic densities all result in more frequent lane-formation clusters compared to platooning clusters.

The choice of the number of AVs and the maximum number of cells used in the definition of a cluster is arbitrary, in our study we used the numbers 4 and 3 because we were simulating roads 100 cells long, and such a definition encapsulates the close proximity requirement and also has a visually significant appeal. Previous works on clustering phenomenon describes the dynamics of non-autonomous heterogeneous vehicle flows such as trucks and buses [@cls1] [@cluster] but we explore such phenomenon in mixed traffic flow of HVs and AVs. In such HV-AV systems, efficient and effective clustering of AVs would imply optimized traffic flow for the entire regime. Thus, we aim to study the AV behaviors that result in strong clustering phenomenon.

We hypothesize that the formation of clusters is natural due to the opportunistic nature of AVs, modeled through both its gap seeking and braking behaviors as we described in Section 3.1{reference-type=”ref” reference=”ss:2”}. In the neighbor aware model of AVs, however, it is actually more rewarding to the AV to trail behind another AV as they can achieve smaller headways. This should lead to the phenomenon of significant and frequent lane formation and platooning as AVs, under this model, have more incentives to change lanes aggressively and overtake HVs to seek and follow a peer. The following section discusses two simulation experiments we conducted to test our hypothesis.

The models with opportunistic behaviors - "opportunistic" and "neighbor aware and opportunistic" - showed bigger cluster sizes and higher clusterability under this regime. When the system was at critical density, the average cluster sizes, number, and clusterability were very similar; this trend also continues under the High density regime.

The only cluster parameter that does show stark difference under the different regimes and models was the average survival times of clsuters. In both the low and critical density regimes, the "neighbor aware and opportunistic" had a longest survival time followed by the "opportunistic" model, "neighbor aware" model, "baseline headway" model and "baseline" model respectively. The high density regime showed all models having the same average survival time - 1200. This survival time gives us a key insight into the nature of the clusters formed in these different AV models; the clusters formed under the baseline model, where the AV was modelled to act indifferently to an HV, were the most transient whereas the models with opportunistic behaviours - "opportunistic" model and "neighbor aware and opportunistic" model - had the most prominent clusters.

Figure [pavavavav]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”pavavavav”} shows how the probability of AV-AV pairs, $P(AV-AV)$ evolve under the different models and regime. This parameter captures how likely it is for an AV to trail another AV. In the low density regime, the $P(AV-AV)$ values under each model converged towards different values with the "neighbor aware" and "opportunistic" models having the highest values and the "neighbor aware and opportunistic" model having the lowest value. Under critical density, however, this trend does not hold and all the $P(AV-AV)$ values converge. While, in the high density regime the models endowed with opportunistic behaviors - "neighbor aware and opportunistic" and "opportunistic" - converged towards similar values and the other models converged towards another value, creating two distinct point of convergence. These results can be explained by analyzing the lane change dynamics of each model. From Figure [combana]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”combana”}, it can be seen that under the low density regime, the number of lane changes made by HVs were comparatively very low when compared to the other two regimes. The lane changing rate, however, for these HVs under the different AV models showed a similar trend under all regimes: HVs when moving with "baseline headway" modeled AV showed the highest lane changing rate and HVs sharing lanes with "neighbor aware and opportunistic" modeled AVs had the lowest lane changing rate.

The AVs with opportunistic behavioral traits - "neighbor aware and opportunistic" and "opportunistic" - had the highest numbers and rates of lane changing in all regimes when compared to other models. Under both low and high density regime, the lane changing rate of these opportunistic AVs converged to similar values while all the rest converged to some other values (with the exception of "baseline" model in low density regime). The dichotomy of convergence is explained by the differences in incentives amongst the various models as the opportunistic agents are aggressive in changing lanes; this dichotomy explains the clusterability results. However, the most interesting trend of lane changing under different AV models is under the critical density regime.

Since, at critical density the road reaches its maximum flow state, AVs under all models show their highest lane change rates. Aside from the "baseline" model of AV, all other models show comparable number of total lane changes. The corresponding HV lane change rates under this regime reveal a key difference in the behavioral dynamics of the AVs. HVs sharing the roads with "neighbor aware and opportunistic" AVs show considerably smaller total number and rates of lane changes compared to all other models. This phenomenon leads to the concept of herding and intelligent herding which is further explored in section 4.2{reference-type=”ref” reference=”exp2”}.


\

These results do verify our initial hypothesis behind the clustering phenomenon discussed in Section 4{reference-type=”ref” reference=”clf”} - neighbor aware and opportunistic AV agents benefit the most from trailing other AVs (due to smaller headways) resulting in collective behavior that lead to strong and prominent clustering phenomenon. Since such self-organization also depend on the availability of empty cells for lane changing, this explains why at lower occupancy states the clustering phenomenon for both opportunistic and neighbor aware and opportunistic models are stronger compared to high occupancy states, where there are not many opportunities available to change lanes. Thus, in high occupancy states the initial distribution of the cars tend to stay roughly uniform, which explains why all the three models in the high density regime had the same average cluster survival time in our experiment. It is to be noted that the survival time for this case was the same as the total simulation time as expected.

Our results prove that prominent clustering is not a random phenomenon but is unique to systems involving opportunistic intelligent agents that are capable of recognition. In context of our experiment, it means that if AVs are aware of other AVs under ideal occupancy states - low to critical density - they would engage in collective behavior without any centralized command and display clustering phenomenon as long as they share common incentive of being opportunistic. This implies that is possible to design AV behavior that can form self-organized clusters.

Equilibrium Relation and Dynamics Under Varying Traffic Densities

For this experiment, we again consider the five different models of AV: "neighbor aware", opportunistic, neighbor aware and opportunistic, baseline and baseline headway, but unlike the first experiment, in this experiment we increase our system density linearly till it reaches the jam density. We allow the simulation to run for a fixed number of time steps (100) for each system density before incrementing it. In our experiment, we increase the system density by $0.03$ on a normalized scale where jam density corresponds to $1$. The trajectory of this system density evolution in the density phase space is determined by $P(AV)$, where state trajectory is a straight line with slope $\frac{\rho_{AV}}{1-\rho_{AV}}$ (see Figure 4{reference-type=”ref” reference=”fig:lane”}). Throughout the simulation, we track this evolution of system density and system throughput (i.e. flux).

Results

In this section we introduce the results from this simulation experiment. We compare the flux of mixed traffic flow in all scenarios (Figures [cfh]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”cfh”} (a), (d), (g), (j), and (m)), the total number of lane changes (Figures [cfh]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”cfh”} (b), (e), (h), (k), and (n)), and the lane change rates in all scenarios (Figures [cfh]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”cfh”} (c), (f), (i), (l), and (o)). For this experiment, we have redefined Lane Change Rate in a spatial context in the following way. \(\text{Lane Change Rate of Vehicle} = \frac{\text{Number of Lane Changes made by Vehicles}}{\text{Number of Vehicles}} \label{lcr}\)

Our results show that the AV models endowed with opportunistic traits perform the best overall. It also shows that when such opportunistic agents also have cognition - the "neighbor aware and opportunistic" model - they have the best overall throughput and the "neighbor aware" model having the third highest overall throughput. This improvement of flow-density relation is attributed to the clustering phenomenon which in turn are results of the behavior design; more prominent and longer lasting the clusters are, the better performance boost to the overall traffic. This clustering phenomenon seems to have aided the flow of HVs as clustering AVs tend to move faster opening space for the trailing HVs who can more consistently maintain their maximum speeds.

These findings argue that the clustering phenomenon does indeed improve traffic flow by allowing opportunistic neighbor aware AVs to seek and trail one another and maintain shorter headways leading to higher speeds for the AV class and more open space for the HV class, which they may use to travel at higher speeds.





\

The total number of lane changes over the simulation period as we add more vehicles into the road gives us some key insights into affect of different behavior design on overall traffic. Both variations of the "baseline" models show AVs making one third of all lane changes as expected due to the proportion of such vehicles in the mix. "Opportunistic" model of AVs showed approximately the same number of lane changes as HVs, while the "Neighbor Aware and Opportunistic" model had AVs switching lanes almost twice as more than HVs, and the "Neighbor Aware" model had initially switched lanes faster and more frequently than HVs but later having significantly lower number than HVs. The increased frequency of lane changing by the "Neighbor Aware and Opportunistic" and "Opportunistic" models are obvious as both these models demonstrate aggressive lane changing tactics. Neighbor aware non-opportunistic AVs had lower rates compared to the opportunistic models but higher than the "baseline" models (See Figure [cffh]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”cffh”} (a)) even with the same lane changing probability as the "baseline" models because these agents were rewarded to trail AVs and hence were more frequently changed lanes when AVs where nearby (in cluster situations).

Figures [cfh]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”cfh”} (c), (f), (i), (l), and (o) show another key finding of our study. In the "baseline" and "baseline headway" model cases, the Lane Change Rate (See Eqn. [lcr]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”lcr”}) for both the vehicle classes - AV and HV - and the overall general vehicle class were roughly the same as expected. The "neighbor aware" model showed AVs having higher lane change rates at lower densities since they were changing lanes more often at those densities due to the higher likelihood of encountering neighboring AVs (See Figure [pavavavav]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”pavavavav”}). Naturally, when AVs were only behaving opportunistically, they had the highest lane change rates, however, it is the comparatively low rates of HVs that is intriguing. AVs movement with respect to HVs in this scenario is very similar to those of shepherd dogs herding sheep. They keep the HVs in check by regulating the flow of traffic making sure the isotropic flow is as smooth as possible by keeping the disrupting agents - HVs - from changing lanes. This sort of behavior is by design as AVs are trying to optimize their transit by opportunistic means leading to this lane changing phenomenon, we call herding.

This herding phenomenon is further accentuated when the opportunistic agents are also cognitive, i.e. in the "neighbor aware and opportunistic" model. The lane change rate for this model is lower than that of the purely "opportunistic" model; this can be explained using a similar analogy - when AVs are "neighbor aware", their movement with respect to HVs are also very similar to those of shepherd dogs herding sheep with the novel addition that these shepherds are now are aware of each other which means that they do not have to move around as often because they recognize the presence of other shepherds and acknowledge their common objective. The AVs, in practice, keep the HVs in check by forming prominent self-organized clusters which prevent the HVs from changing lanes. We call this phenomena intelligent herding.

Such behavioral based lane change dynamics have severe implications on safety, road design and fuel consumption and will be described in detail in the next section.

Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate behaviors of mixed traffic flow of AVs and HVs through simulation experiments. The major purpose is to understand the relation between opportunistic behaviors of AV and formation of clusters and lanes without centralized control. For this purpose, we propose a new cellular automata model to account for potential behaviors difference between AVs and HVs. Notably, the opportunistic character of AVs are modeled through their gap seeking behaviors and awareness of neighboring vehicle types.Simulation experiments were conducted in two scenarios, which compare the clustering process and traffic flow performance with the three models of AV at fixed densities and linearly increasing densities.

One major finding of this research, which is intriguing, is the self-organized formation of AV clusters and lanes without any centralized control. Such phenomena are observed in our simulation experiments even no dedicated AV lane is in place. This may suggest clustering as an intrinsic property of mixed HV and AV flow. We postulate such self-organized phenomena is due to the incentives that AVs perceive to seek and partner with peer AVs. When AVs are opportunistic, such effect is reinforced, as seen in our experiment. If the postulation is confirmed to be true through further simulation or field experiments, it may suggest the possibility of regulating mixed HV-AV flow through designing or inducing decentralized incentives, instead of performing centralized coordination.Our research also compares the mixed flux in all scenarios, and the positive effect of the clustering phenomenon is confirmed.

The findings of this paper may serve as initial evidence to the self-organization in mixed flow of AVs and HVs. We recognized the proposed model is by no means realistic in the quantitative sense. In addition, as AVs haven’t been launched in large scale in the real world and existing data is limited, their opportunistic behavior just represent on possibility we deem likely. In future works, as more data become available, it is desirable to model the decision-making of AVs more realistically, and further verify the finding in this paper.

Traditional Cellular Automata Rules

  • Rule A: In our model $v_{l’}$ is defined as: $v_{l’} = min(v_{max}, d_{back})$. Here, $v_{l’}$ represents the potential speed the vehicle can attain if it switches lane from lane $l$ to lane $l’$ and $d_{j}$ is the number of empty cells in front of vehicle in lane $l$. The incentive criterion dictates that the vehicle will switch lanes only if: \(v_{l'} > d_{j}\)

  • Rule B: \(\label{eq:3} d_{back} > v_{prev}\) Once an incentive to switch lanes have been established, the safety criterion implies that the vehicle looks for the car that would be behind it in the target lane, $l’$ Then, it is required that the distance to the previous car, $d_{back}$, is greater than the speed of the previous car, $v_{prev}$ in the target lane to avoid any collision while switching lanes.

  • Rule C:\(\centering l \rightarrow l'\) After the conditions for both Rules A an B are satisfied, the vehicle has a probability, $p_l$, of switching lanes. This probability is associated with the driver’s reluctance to switch lanes even though it would deem plausible; thereby making the model stochastic and realistic. The vehicle changes lane once all these conditions are met.

  • Rule 1 and 2: If $v_j < v_{max}$ and the distance to the next vehicle, $d_j$, is greater than $v_j + 1$, the vehicle accelerates and the new speed, $v_j$, is: \(v_j = min(min(v_j + 1, v_{max}), d_j)\) Rule 1 ensures that the vehicle accelerates linearly till it reaches its maximum speed, $v_{max}$, while Rule 2 also makes sure that there is no collision.\

  • Rule 3:\(v_j = v_j - 1 \label{eq:3}\) The vehicle may slow down randomly with a vehicle type dependent braking probability, $p_s$, and the new speed, $v_{j}$ would be given by Eqn. [eq:3]{reference-type=”ref” reference=”eq:3”}. This rule is included to model erratic driver behaviour in HVs and the lack thereof in AVs; it makes the simulation reflect realistic traffic flow by taking away total determinism from the model.

  • Rule 4: The vehicle moves $v_j$ cells from its initial position in the upstream direction. \(x_j = x_j + v_j \label{eq:4}\)
